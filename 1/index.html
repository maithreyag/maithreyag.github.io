Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

CS180 · Project 1 · Maithreya G. · back to portfolio

Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in color a decade before color film was practical: each exposure was shot three times, through a blue, a green, and a red filter, onto one glass plate. Recovering the photograph means cutting the scan into its three frames and undoing the camera's shift between exposures. Every scan is converted to floating point first, so 8-bit JPEGs and 16-bit TIFFs behave identically, then split into thirds of height floor(H/3), in the order blue, green, red from top to bottom. Blue stays fixed and the other two are aligned to it. Offsets are applied with np.roll, a circular shift that wraps the displaced rows and columns to the far edge, and every comparison is scored on the interior alone, with 10% of the height and width trimmed from each side so that plate borders and margin notes can't drive the match. A brute-force search handles the small scans; the full-size TIFFs need an image pyramid. Offsets are written (dy, dx), rows then columns, relative to blue.

Two details apply to every image on this page. The matching score is normalized cross-correlation, and it is computed on gradient-magnitude edge maps instead of raw pixels. I explain both in the next section, and the Emir plate at the bottom shows why the second one is needed.

Single-scale alignment

The single-scale search is exhaustive. For each candidate offset (dy, dx) within ±15 pixels in both directions, which is 31 × 31 = 961 candidates, I roll the moving frame by that offset, score it against the fixed blue frame, and keep the highest-scoring one. The search itself is simple, so most of the work is in choosing the score.

Step 1: choosing a score, L2 vs. NCC

The obvious score is the L2 norm, the sum of squared differences between the rolled frame I and the reference R, summed over the cropped interior C:

L2(dy, dx)  =  Σ  [ I(y-dy, x-dx) - R(y, x) ]²
           (y,x) ∈ C
Minimized over offsets. The index shift follows np.roll's convention, where rolling by +dy moves content down.

L2 measures agreement in brightness. It is smallest when the two frames hold the same pixel values, which is not the same as holding the same structures. The frames come from different color filters, so a surface that is bright through the blue filter can be dark through the red one. This mismatch shows up at every candidate offset, and it is often larger than the difference between a correct and an incorrect alignment, so it hides the signal the search needs.

Normalized cross-correlation avoids this. Flatten both patches into vectors a and b, subtract each one's mean, and take the cosine of the angle between them:

a' = a - mean(a)          b' = b - mean(b)

                    a' · b'
NCC(dy, dx)  =  ───────────────
                  |a'| · |b'|
Maximized over offsets. Subtracting the mean cancels a brightness offset between the two filters, and dividing by the norms cancels a contrast scale factor. The result is the cosine of the angle between the two centered vectors, in [−1, 1], so it measures whether the bright and dark regions of the two frames move together.

Step 2: score edges, not pixels

NCC cancels an additive brightness shift and a multiplicative contrast factor, but it still fails when a region's brightness order flips between filters. Something bright in blue and dark in red inverts the correlation rather than just offsetting it. To avoid this, each frame is replaced by its gradient magnitude before any scoring happens:

gy, gx  =  np.gradient(I)

E       =  sqrt( gy² + gx² )
An edge sits in the same place in all three frames regardless of how each filter renders the brightness on either side of it, so the edge maps are what the three exposures agree on.

The order of these two operations matters. The gradient is taken on the full frame and the 10% border crop is applied afterward, so the one-sided differences NumPy computes at the frame boundary get discarded before they can affect the score.

Every offset reported on this page, from both the single-scale search and the pyramid, is therefore the argmax of NCC over cropped edge maps. Running the same search on raw pixels instead produces the Emir failure shown at the bottom of the page.

Results

Multi-scale alignment

The TIFFs are about ten times larger per side, and their displacements grow with them: melons moves its red frame 177 pixels, well outside a ±15 window. Widening the window doesn't help, since its cost grows as the square of its radius while each candidate now scores a hundred times as many pixels.

Instead of widening the window, the pyramid shrinks the displacement until it fits the window we already have. The routine is recursive, and each call does four things:

  1. Base case. If the frame's shorter side is at most 100 pixels, run the exhaustive ±15 search from above and return its answer. At this size a 177-pixel displacement has become a few pixels, so ±15 is wide enough.
  2. Downsample. Otherwise halve both the moving frame and the reference with rescale(…, 0.5, anti_aliasing=True). Anti-aliasing is important here, since decimating without it folds high-frequency detail into false low-frequency structure, and the coarse levels are where the search is most easily misled.
  3. Recurse, then rescale the estimate. Align the half-size pair by calling itself, then double the offset it returns. A displacement of d at half scale is 2d here.
  4. Refine. That doubled estimate is correct to within the rounding it picked up on the way down, so search only ±2 around it, which is 25 candidates, and return the best.

This keeps the cost down. The 961-candidate window runs exactly once, at the coarsest level where the images are tiny, and every level above it costs 25 comparisons. Five halvings take melons' 177-pixel red displacement down to about 5 pixels (177 / 25 ≈ 5.5), well inside ±15.

On the three small JPEGs the pyramid reproduces the brute-force answers. Cathedral and monastery come out byte-for-byte identical to the single-scale images above, so those pairs are the same file. Only tobolsk differs, by one pixel in green, (3, 3) against (3, 2), which is invisible in the output. Every level of the pyramid uses the same NCC-on-edge-maps score described in the previous section.

Three more from the collection

Three plates I picked from the Library of Congress Prokudin-Gorskii collection and ran through the same pyramid without changes. All three are small JPEG scans, so their displacements are small and the pyramid bottoms out after one or two halvings. Each title links to its catalog record: Kurmy, four people seated on a carpet, and man with camel loaded with packs.

Failure case: Emir

Emir is the plate that motivates the edge map from Step 2. Running the same pyramid on raw pixels instead of edge maps, with the same NCC, the same window and the same recursion, and only the gradient step removed, fails badly.

His robe is saturated blue, the brightest object in the blue frame and nearly the darkest in the red one. This is the case NCC cannot handle. Mean subtraction and normalization deal with a frame that is uniformly brighter or more contrasty, but here the brightness ordering is inverted over the largest object in the picture, so the correlation goes negative at the offset where the robe actually lines up. The search then prefers an offset that puts the robe somewhere else. The red offset lands at (118, −542) and the Emir appears twice.

Scoring sqrt(gy² + gx²) removes the inversion. The gradient discards the absolute brightness on either side of a boundary and keeps only the fact that a boundary is there, so the outline of the robe sits in the same place in all three exposures no matter which filter renders it light or dark. The red offset moves to (107, 40) and the image aligns correctly.

Emir is the only plate where this makes a large difference, but I use the edge map for every image rather than switching it on for this one. It costs one np.gradient call per frame and did not make any other result worse.
